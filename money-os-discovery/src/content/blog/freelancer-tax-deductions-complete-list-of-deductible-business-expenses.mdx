You can deduct virtually any expense that is ordinary, necessary, and directly tied to earning freelance income, from a laptop you bought for client work to the coffee you sip during a client meeting. Below is a complete, organized list of every category the IRS allows freelancers to write off, plus the items that are off-limits and common pitfalls to avoid. Knowing exactly which costs qualify lets you lower your taxable profit, keep more cash in hand, and stay audit-ready. For example, a $1,200 ergonomic chair purchased for long-hour coding sessions qualifies, while a $300 gym membership does not. The list is broken into five sections - office and equipment, travel and meals, professional services, home office utilities, and non-deductible items - so you can quickly spot where each of your receipts belongs and set up your bookkeeping in Money OS for effortless tax-ready reporting.

## Common Office and Equipment Deductions

You can deduct the hardware, software, and everyday supplies you need to run your freelance business, and the IRS treats those costs just like any other ordinary and necessary expense. Keep receipts for everything from a $1,199 MacBook Pro to a $45 monthly Adobe Creative Cloud subscription, because each line item reduces your taxable income dollar for dollar.

**Hardware** - Desktops, laptops, tablets, external monitors, and peripherals are fully deductible in the year you place them in service if they cost $2,500 or less; anything above that must be depreciated over five years. For example, a $2,399 Dell XPS laptop bought in March can be written off immediately, while a $3,200 27-inch monitor requires a 5-year MACRS schedule (roughly $640 per year).

**Software & SaaS** - Subscription fees are 100 % deductible as they recur each month. Track each payment: $29/mo for Canva Pro, $99/mo for QuickBooks Online, or $15/mo for a premium VPN. Annual licenses, like a $199 Photoshop perpetual license, are also fully deductible.

**Office Supplies** - Pens, printer ink, paper, and a $79 ergonomic mouse all qualify. Even a $250 ergonomic chair can be expensed outright if it falls under the $2,500 safe-harbor limit.

**Consumables & Minor Repairs** - Replace a broken keyboard for $45 or purchase a $12 pack of USB-C cables; these are ordinary supplies, not capital assets, and can be deducted in full.

**Phone & Internet** - If you use a dedicated line for business, the entire bill is deductible. For a shared plan, allocate the business percentage (e.g., 60 % of a $80/month bill = $48 deductible).

## Travel, Meals, and Client-Facing Expenses

You can deduct 100 % of airfare, train tickets, rideshares, and mileage when the trip's primary purpose is business, and you can claim 50 % of meals that you eat with clients or while on the road. Anything that directly supports a client meeting, conference presentation, or on-site work qualifies, as long as you keep itemized receipts and a brief purpose note.

**Airfare and ground transport** - A freelance web designer who flew from Austin to New York for a $2,500 client pitch can write off the $350 ticket plus $45 Uber rides to the venue. If you drive your own car, use the IRS standard rate of $0.655 per mile (2024) and record the odometer start- and end-readings; a 150-mile round-trip equals $98.25 deductible mileage.

**Lodging** - Hotel costs are fully deductible when the night is required for business. A copywriter staying three nights at $180 per night in Chicago can claim $540. Remember to separate any personal expenses (e.g., minibar) from the room charge.

**Meals** - The 50 % rule applies to meals that are "business-related." A marketing freelancer who takes a client to a $120 dinner can deduct $60. Keep the receipt, note the attendees, and describe the business purpose (e.g., "project scope discussion"). Alcohol is included in the same 50 % limit, but you cannot claim it separately.

**Client-facing supplies** - Branded swag, printed proposals, and coffee for a client workshop are fully deductible. For example, $75 spent on custom notebooks and $30 on catering for a half-day strategy session can be written off 100 %.

**What to avoid** - Do not claim personal vacations, family meals, or entertainment that isn't directly tied to a business discussion. If a trip mixes personal and business days, only deduct expenses for the business portion and allocate the rest to personal use.

## Professional Services and Subscriptions

You can deduct the fees you pay for accountants, legal counsel, and any subscription services that keep your freelance business running. The IRS treats these professional services as ordinary and necessary business expenses, so they reduce your taxable income dollar for dollar. 

A typical freelance web developer might spend $250 / year on a domain-registration service like Namecheap, $120 / year on a design-resource library such as Envato Elements, and $500 / year on accounting software like QuickBooks Self-Employed. All three line items are fully deductible on Schedule C. If you hire a tax professional to file your return, the fee - often $150-$300 for a simple freelance schedule - also qualifies. 

Legal fees are deductible when they protect or advance your business. For example, drafting a client contract with a lawyer costs $300 and can be written off. However, personal legal matters (e.g., divorce) are not eligible. 

When evaluating subscriptions, ask two questions: (1) Is the service used exclusively for business? (2) Does it generate revenue or support client delivery? If the answer is yes, record the expense in the "Professional Services" category of Money OS and set a budget alert to avoid overspending.

## Home Office and Utilities

Deducting your home office expenses can significantly reduce your taxable income, but it requires careful tracking. You can deduct a portion of your rent or mortgage interest, property taxes, utilities (electricity, gas, water), and even homeowners insurance. The key is to determine the "exclusive and regular" use of a specific area in your home solely for business. This isn't for a corner of your living room where you occasionally answer emails. It must be a dedicated space.

To calculate your deduction, you'll need to determine the square footage of your home office and divide it by the total square footage of your home. For example, if your office is 100 sq ft and your home is 1,000 sq ft, you can deduct 10% of your eligible home expenses. If your total rent or mortgage interest is $2,000 per month, your deductible portion for the office would be $200. Similarly, if your monthly utility bill is $300, you can deduct $30.

You have two options for calculating this deduction: the simplified method or the regular method. The simplified method allows you to deduct $5 per square foot, up to a maximum of 300 square feet (a $1,500 deduction). This is straightforward but caps your potential savings. The regular method, while more involved, often yields a larger deduction if your actual expenses are higher. You'll need detailed records of all your home expenses, just as you would for [tracking business expenses](/blog/how-to-track-business-expenses-freelancer). Remember, the IRS scrutinizes home office deductions, so keep meticulous records and ensure your space meets the "exclusive and regular use" criteria. ## What Cannot Be Deducted and How to Avoid Mistakes

You cannot deduct personal expenses, non-business entertainment, or the cost of commuting from your freelance taxes, and the fastest way to avoid costly errors is to separate every transaction at the source. Set up a dedicated business account, use a credit card solely for work purchases, and record the purpose of each spend in real time.

Personal items - clothing, groceries, gym memberships, or a Netflix subscription - are 100 % nondeductible even if you claim them as "comfort" while working. The IRS also disallows any entertainment that is not directly tied to a business purpose; a dinner with a friend or a concert ticket for yourself does not qualify, regardless of the "networking" rationale. Commuting costs between your home and a regular work location are considered personal travel, so mileage, tolls, and parking for that leg are off-limits.

Common pitfalls:

- **Mixing accounts** - When personal and business expenses share a bank account, you'll spend hours re-categorizing and risk missing deductions. 
- **Failing to document** - A receipt without a brief note (client name, project, date) is useless if the IRS audits you. 
- **Claiming 100 % for mixed-use assets** - A laptop used 60 % for client work and 40 % for streaming must be prorated; claiming the full amount will trigger a red flag. 
- **Over-claiming home-office space** - Only the portion of your home used exclusively and regularly for business qualifies; a bedroom that doubles as a guest room does not.

## FAQ

### Can I deduct my coworking space membership as a freelancer?

Yes, you can deduct the full cost of a coworking membership if the space is used exclusively for business activities. The expense is treated like a home office rent deduction and should be reported on Schedule C.

### How much of my internet bill is tax-deductible?

You can deduct the portion of your internet bill that is used for work, typically 50% to 100% depending on your usage. Keep a log of business versus personal use and apply that percentage to the total monthly charge.

### Are software subscriptions like Adobe Creative Cloud eligible for freelancer tax deductions?

Yes, subscription fees for tools you use to generate income, such as Adobe Creative Cloud, are fully deductible as ordinary and necessary business expenses. Record the subscription cost and the date you started using it for client work.

### Do I need receipts for every expense to claim a deduction?

The IRS requires documentation for each deduction, and receipts are the most reliable proof. If a receipt is unavailable, a bank statement, invoice, or a detailed written record can suffice, but keep it for at least three years.

### What records should I keep for audit-proof freelancer tax deductions?

Maintain digital or paper copies of receipts, invoices, bank and credit-card statements, and a mileage log if you claim vehicle expenses. Organize them by category (office, travel, software, etc.) and store them for a minimum of seven years in case of an audit.
